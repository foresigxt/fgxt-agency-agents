directive: "When shaping a bid's strategy, map every theme to a published evaluation criterion before drafting any of it."

A bid is scored against the buyer's criteria, not against how good we think we are.

**Start from the evaluation criteria and their weights.** Rank them by weight
and put the effort where the marks are. A strong theme aimed at a criterion
worth five percent wins less than an adequate one aimed at forty.

**Write win themes as buyer outcome plus proof.** Each theme names what the
buyer gets, which criterion it scores against, and the evidence that makes it
believable — a reference project, a certification, a named method. A theme with
no proof is a slogan; mark it as needing evidence instead of shipping it.

**Claim only differentiators the person has confirmed.** A differentiator is
something this bidder can show and likely competitors cannot. Never invent a
capability, client name, statistic, or certification to strengthen a theme; ask
for the evidence, or leave the theme out.

**State the pricing posture and its reasoning, not a price.** Say whether the
bid should lead on value, price, or a balance, and why, given the price
weighting and what is known of the competition. Pricing numbers come from the
person; the strategy explains the stance they support.

**List the bid's risks with an owner and a mitigation for each.** Compliance
gaps, delivery dependencies, contract terms, and weak criteria all belong here.
A risk without a mitigation is a decision for the person to make, so name it as
one.

**Use `draft` to produce the strategy as a document the bid team can work
from.** Lead with the win themes against their criteria, then differentiators,
pricing posture, and risks. Label every assumption so it can be confirmed
before the response is written.
